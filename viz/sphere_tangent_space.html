Geometry of unit spheres, projectors and products of spheres

Why every velocity on a sphere is perpendicular to the position

If a point is forced to stay at distance 1 from the origin, then the only directions it can move are the ones that don't change that distance. This page explains why that is the same as ⟨ż, z⟩ = 0, and why it matters for dynamics on spheres.

Differentiating ⟨z(t), z(t)⟩ = 1 along a curve on Sn−1 gives ⟨ż, z⟩ = 0, so the tangent space at z is the hyperplane TzSn−1 = {v : ⟨v, z⟩ = 0}.

The picture in one sentence

Imagine standing on a ball of radius 1, with a string tied from the ball's center to your feet. Walk anywhere you like, but the string stays taut and the same length. Your velocity can't have any part pointing along the string, because that would stretch it or slacken it. So your velocity must point sideways, perpendicular to the string. The string is z. Your velocity is ż. "Perpendicular" is the equation ⟨ż, z⟩ = 0.

1. A circle first (n = 2)

On the circle you can see everything at once. Below, z is the point on the circle and v is a candidate velocity. Split v into a part along z (orange) and a part along the tangent line (green). The orange part is exactly ⟨v, z⟩. It is the rate at which you move away from the center.

Drag the point z or the tip of v. Then drag the step size ε to see where one small step in direction v lands. Only when the orange part is zero does the step stay (to first order) on the circle.

2. Why the derivative gives exactly this

Everything on the sphere satisfies ⟨z, z⟩ = ‖z‖² = 1. Now let z(t) move along any smooth curve that stays on the sphere. The quantity ⟨z(t), z(t)⟩ is the constant 1 for all t, so its rate of change is zero. Computing that rate with the product rule gives the claim:

d/dt ⟨z, z⟩ = ⟨ż, z⟩ + ⟨z, ż⟩
Product rule for the inner product. Each of the two copies of z gets differentiated once.
= 2⟨ż, z⟩
The inner product is symmetric, so both terms are equal.
d/dt ⟨z, z⟩ = d/dt (1) = 0
On the sphere ⟨z, z⟩ never changes.
⟹ ⟨ż, z⟩ = 0
Same quantity, two ways: 2⟨ż, z⟩ = 0. Velocity is perpendicular to position.

There is a second way to read the same line. Since ‖z‖ = 1, the number ⟨ż, z⟩ is the speed at which you are moving outward (velocity component along the unit vector z). The equation says: outward speed = 0. This is the string argument from the top of the page in symbols.

That shows every velocity of a curve on the sphere lies in the hyperplane {v : ⟨v, z⟩ = 0}. The reverse also holds: for any v in that hyperplane, the great circle γ(s) = cos(s)·z + sin(s)·v/‖v‖ stays on the sphere and has velocity v/‖v‖ at s = 0. So the hyperplane is exactly the set of possible velocities. Try it in the next figure.

3. The same thing on a ball (n = 3)

In three dimensions the "hyperplane" is an ordinary plane touching the sphere at one point (blue patch). The string z is its normal. The orange curve is a great circle through z; its velocity at z (orange arrow) always lies in the blue plane, whichever direction you choose. The red arrow shows a velocity with an outward component: it sticks out of the plane, and its inner product with z is no longer 0.

In general z lives in ℝⁿ. The single condition ⟨v, z⟩ = 0 removes one dimension, leaving an (n−1)-dimensional hyperplane. That matches the dimension of the sphere Sn−1 itself. You have n−1 independent directions to move in, and one forbidden direction (the radial one).

4. Why this matters: forces must be projected

Suppose an energy E(z) pushes z with some force u (for instance u = −∇E), and z is required to stay on the sphere. The force usually has a radial part, which would push you off the sphere. The part of the force that can actually move you is the tangent part:

Projz(u) = u − ⟨u, z⟩ z

Subtract the component along z. What remains has ⟨Projz(u), z⟩ = ⟨u,z⟩ − ⟨u,z⟩⟨z,z⟩ = 0, so it lies in the tangent hyperplane. This is the standard form of the Riemannian gradient on a sphere, and it is how oscillator-style dynamics with unit-norm states are written: a force plus a projection that removes the part that would change the length.

Below, the energy is E(z) = −½ zᵀAz with A a symmetric 2×2 matrix, so the force is u = Az. Orange: the raw force. Gray: the radial part that gets thrown away. Green: the tangent part that drives the update z ← normalize(z + η·Projz(Az)). The ring is colored by energy (strong blue = low). The motion stops where the green arrow vanishes: Az is parallel to z, i.e. at an eigenvector of A.

5. The projector Pz = I − zzᵀ

Section 4 already used a recipe, "subtract the part along z". As a matrix, that recipe is Pz = I − zzᵀ. The matrix zzᵀ is a shadow machine: feed it u and it returns (zzᵀ)u = ⟨z, u⟩ z, the piece of u that lies along z. Subtracting it leaves only the piece perpendicular to z.

Four properties, each with a plain meaning:

Symmetric: Pzᵀ = Pz
The shadow is cast straight down (perpendicular), with no slant. Equivalent statement: ⟨P u, w⟩ = ⟨u, P w⟩, so it doesn't matter which of the two vectors you project.
Idempotent: Pz² = Pz
Projecting twice is the same as projecting once. After the first projection the vector is already flat against the tangent line, so there is nothing left to remove. Proof: (I − zzᵀ)² = I − 2zzᵀ + z(zᵀz)zᵀ = I − zzᵀ, using zᵀz = 1.
Pz z = 0
The "string" direction is erased completely: z − ⟨z, z⟩ z = z − z = 0.
⟨v, z⟩ = 0 ⟹ Pz v = v
Tangent vectors have no component along z, so there's nothing to subtract and they pass through unchanged.

Note that all of this relies on ‖z‖ = 1. For a general z you'd need I − zzᵀ/‖z‖².

6. The radial part is invisible after normalization

"Normalize" means: take a vector q ≠ 0 and replace it by q/‖q‖. Geometrically, you slide q along the ray from the origin through q until it hits the unit sphere. Now ask what a push u = r·z + s·t (radial part r z, tangent part s t) does to z:

q = z + u = (1 + r) z + s t
The radial part only changes how far out along z you are. The tangent part moves you sideways.
q/‖q‖ = z if s = 0
With no tangent part, q is a multiple of z. It is on the same ray, so normalization puts it exactly back at z. The radial part changed the length and nothing else.
angle moved = arctan( s / (1 + r) )
With a tangent part, the direction of q is set by the ratio of sideways to outward. The radial part only enters as the scale 1 + r that sets how big a sideways step looks. It never creates motion by itself.

So "only the tangential part moves a point along the sphere" is literally true: set the tangent part to zero and the point cannot move, no matter how large the radial part is. Play with the sliders: with s = 0 nothing moves for any r.

7. Many unit vectors at once: the product manifold

So far there was one vector on one sphere. In practice the state is a collection: M unit vectors z = (z₁, …, zM), each zm ∈ SN−1. Picture M clocks, each with a hand of length 1 (in N dimensions rather than 2). The state of the whole system is the position of all hands at once, and the set of all such states is the product manifold (SN−1)M.

Tangent space = product of tangent spaces. Each hand has its own constraint ⟨zm, zm⟩ = 1, and the constraints don't interact. So each hand can move sideways independently of the others, and the allowed velocities are all tuples (v₁, …, vM) with ⟨vm, zm⟩ = 0 for each m. Count: each block contributes N − 1 free directions, for M(N − 1) in total. That's the MN numbers in the state minus M constraints, one per hand.

The projector acts block by block. Stack the hands into one long vector in ℝMN. The projector onto the tangent space is the block-diagonal matrix P = diag(Pz₁, …, PzM): the m-th block of the output only depends on the m-th block of the input. The off-diagonal blocks are exactly zero, since forces on hand k can't change the length constraint of hand m.

In the setting you pasted, AKOrN uses M = C/N small factors per position (each a short vector of N channels), whereas SFPRM uses one factor per token, N = d, giving the state space (Sd−1)T for T tokens. Same geometry, different choices of M and N.

A concrete case: two circles make a torus

Take N = 2 (each block is a circle) and M = 2. Each state is a pair of angles (θ₁, θ₂), so the product manifold S¹ × S¹ is a torus. Cut it open and you get a square where opposite edges are glued. The dimension is M(N−1) = 2. A tangent vector is just a pair of angular velocities, and the left/right panels are two views of one number pair. 8. Rescaling by 1/√M: all blocks inside one big sphere

You can also forget the block structure and treat the stacked vector (z₁, …, zM) ∈ ℝMN as a single vector. If every block has norm 1, the stacked vector has squared norm 1 + 1 + ⋯ + 1 = M. Dividing by √M makes it a unit vector, so (z₁, …, zM)/√M ∈ SMN−1. This is the embedding.

The embedding is not onto. A generic point of the big sphere has blocks of unequal lengths, and its blocks can't be rescaled to unit without moving it. Only the points where all M blocks have the same norm (necessarily 1/√M) come from the product manifold. That is M − 1 extra equations (‖z₁‖ = ‖z₂‖ = ⋯ = ‖zM‖), which is exactly why the dimension drops from MN − 1 to M(N−1) = MN − 1 − (M−1).

For M = 2 you can see all of it in the plane of block norms (r₁, r₂) = (‖z₁‖, ‖z₂‖). The big sphere says r₁² + r₂² = 1, a quarter circle. The product manifold is the single point on the diagonal r₁ = r₂ = 1/√2.

9. Distance on a sphere: the arc versus the chord

Take two unit vectors u and v. There are two natural ways to ask how far apart they are:

Geodesic distance: walk along the sphere, never leaving it. The length of the shortest walk is the angle θ = ∠(u, v) (radius 1, so arc length = angle). Chordal distance: tunnel straight through the ball. That length is ‖u − v‖.

The squared chord is cheap to compute and tied to the angle by one line of algebra. Since ‖u − v‖² = ‖u‖² − 2⟨u, v⟩ + ‖v‖² = 2 − 2⟨u, v⟩:

1 − ⟨u, v⟩ = 1 − cos θ = ½ ‖u − v‖²

This is the quantity used in the halting test you quoted. It needs no arccos, it grows monotonically with θ on [0, π] (so a threshold on it is a threshold on the angle), and for small angles 1 − cos θ ≈ θ²/2. Drag u and v in the figure. The right plot compares the chordal quantity with the squared angle over 2: they agree near 0 and then separate, with the chordal one saturating at 2 when the vectors are opposite.

10. Geodesics, Exp, Log and Slerp

A geodesic is the sphere's version of a straight line: a path that never turns. On a sphere those are great circles, the circles you get by slicing the ball with a plane through the center (equator, meridians, flight routes). Starting at u and heading in the unit tangent direction e (so ⟨e, u⟩ = 0, ‖e‖ = 1), you trace

Expu(t e) = cos(t) u + sin(t) e

Why this works: u ⟂ e gives ‖cos(t)u + sin(t)e‖² = cos²t + sin²t = 1, so the path stays on the sphere. At t = 0 it is at u, with velocity e, and t is the angle travelled. Nothing is stretched: the tangent vector t e is "wrapped" onto the sphere with its length preserved as arc length.

Log is the inverse: unwrap. Given a target v, how do you find the tangent vector that wraps onto it?

v = cos θ · u + sin θ · e
Decompose v into its part along u and its part in the tangent space. By the formula for Exp, the weights are cos θ and sin θ.
Pu v = v − cos θ · u = sin θ · e,  so  ‖Pu v‖² = 1 − cos²θ = sin²θ
Project away the u part (Section 5). What's left points in the direction e and has length sin θ.
e = Pu v / ‖Pu v‖,   θ = atan2(‖Pu v‖, ⟨u, v⟩)
Direction from the projection. The angle needs both sin θ (the length of P v) and cos θ (= ⟨u, v⟩), since sin alone can't tell θ from π − θ.
Logu v = θ · Pu v / ‖Pu v‖
The tangent vector in direction e whose length is the arc length θ, longer than the shadow sin θ.

Slerp (spherical linear interpolation) is just walking a fraction of the way along that geodesic: Slerp(u, v; t) = Expu(t · Logu v) = [sin((1−t)θ) u + sin(tθ) v] / sin θ. It is undefined at antipodal pairs: for v = −u we have sin θ = 0 and Pu v = 0, so there is no direction to normalize. Every half great circle from u to −u has the same length π, so the shortest arc isn't unique.

In the figure the sphere is rotated so that u is always at the top, and its tangent line is the horizontal line. The green point is where the projection Pu v lands. The orange point is where Logu v lands. It is farther out than green, because the arc is longer than its shadow. Drag v towards the bottom and watch the green point collapse to the origin of the line.

11. Retractions: a cheaper way to wrap

Computing Exp needs a sine and a cosine, and it moves exactly along the great circle. A retraction Rz : TzSn−1 → Sn−1 is any cheaper map that does the same job approximately: it must satisfy Rz(0) = z (no step, no move) and have the identity as derivative at 0 (small steps move in the requested direction at the requested speed). So it agrees with Exp to first order and may differ after that.

The retraction used by AKOrN and nGPT is the one you already met in Section 6: add the step, then normalize, Rz(v) = Π(z + v) with Π(q) = q/‖q‖. For a tangent v:

‖z + v‖² = ‖z‖² + 2⟨z, v⟩ + ‖v‖² = 1 + ‖v‖²
The cross term vanishes because v is tangent (⟨z, v⟩ = 0). This is the Pythagorean theorem for the right triangle with legs z and v.
Π(z + v) = cos φ · z + sin φ · v/‖v‖,  tan φ = ‖v‖
Divide by √(1+‖v‖²): cos φ = 1/√(1+‖v‖²) and sin φ = ‖v‖/√(1+‖v‖²). The triangle has "adjacent" side 1 (the string z) and "opposite" side ‖v‖ (the step), so the angle at the origin is arctan ‖v‖.
Expz(v) travels ‖v‖,  Π(z + v) travels arctan ‖v‖
Same great circle, same direction, different distance along it. Since arctan x ≤ x with equality to first order, they agree for small steps.

Two consequences. First, normalization can never move more than a quarter turn: arctan ‖v‖ < π/2 however big the step. Second, the three operations (Exp, retraction, Slerp) all walk the same great circle and differ only in how a step parameter is converted into an angle. To land at the same point at angle α:

Exp: step length ‖v‖ = α  ·  retraction: ‖v‖ = tan α (needs α < π/2)  ·  Slerp toward a target at angle θ: t = α/θ.

That's why step sizes can be traded between them exactly. In the figure, drag the step length τ. Green is Exp (it lands at angle τ). Orange is normalization (it lands at angle arctan τ, a smaller angle). Tick the box to give the retraction the converted step tan τ instead. It then lands on exactly the same point, and so does Slerp with t = τ/θ.

12. Riemannian gradients and Riemannian gradient descent

Take a smooth function f defined on all of ℝⁿ, and look at it only on the sphere. Its ordinary gradient ∇f(z) points in the direction of steepest increase in the whole space. But on the sphere you can only move tangentially (Section 1), so the useful part is the tangent part (Section 5):

grad f(z) = Pz ∇f(z)

Riemannian gradient descent is then "take a tangent step downhill and wrap it back onto the sphere": z ← Rz(−γ grad f(z)), where R is any retraction, for instance normalization.

The two potentials from the text, side by side:

f(z) = −⟨c, z⟩  ⟹  grad f = −Pz c
Linear potential (AKOrN's stimulus). ∇f = −c is constant, so grad f is just minus the tangent part of c. Descent pulls z toward the direction of c. The pull has size ‖c‖ sin θ: it is weak when z is nearly opposite to c and also vanishes at θ = π, an unstable standstill.
f(z) = ½ ∠(z, x)²  ⟹  grad f = −Logz x
Geodesic potential (SFPRM's injection). The force is the unrolled arc to x: a vector pointing straight along the shortest path with length θ, the true distance. The pull grows with distance instead of fading out when far away. It is undefined at the antipode.

Try both below. Set x (the target, or the direction of c with ‖c‖ = 1) and z by dragging. The right-hand plot shows the size of the pull as a function of the angle θ: sin θ for the linear potential, θ for the geodesic one. Starting far away, the geodesic force is much stronger. Near the target they coincide, since sin θ ≈ θ. Use a large step γ in the geodesic mode and watch the retraction overshoot: near the target, a step arctan(γθ) exceeds θ whenever γ > 1 and ends up on the other side of x (it overshoots more and more once γ > 2).
