New method: an energy-based SFPRM with a codebook memory

The dictionary has one row where the two models disagree: AKOrN can have an energy, SFPRM never does. The new method closes that gap from the SFPRM side. Each iteration takes one Riemannian gradient step on an explicit energy, so every step is an AKOrN step whose drive is −grad E, and the halting residual becomes the size of that gradient.

The figures below build the intuition in four steps: what an energy does to the dynamics near a fixed point, what the memory term does to a single cell, a small Sudoku solved by descending an energy, and what the trained models show.

1 · An energy removes rotation, but not stiffness

Near a halted state the error e evolves as e ← (I − ηM)e. For a gradient step, M is the Hessian of E: symmetric, so its eigenvalues are real and the error shrinks without turning. A learned map like SFPRM's adds an antisymmetric part ωR, which rotates the error. Rotation pushes the eigenvalues off the real axis and lets the energy rise along the way. The stiffness slider makes one mode steeper than the other, which is what the trained energy models turned out to be.

2 · One cell: the memory term and its β knob

With prototypes of equal norm, eβ⟨ξk, z⟩ is a von Mises–Fisher density on the sphere, so the memory energy is minus the log of a mixture centred on the prototypes (green ring). Here a single cell lives on a circle with four digit prototypes. The blue arrow is the pull from the rest of the puzzle, standing in for injection and attention. At small β the mixture is almost flat and the pull decides where the cell goes. At large β each prototype digs its own well and the cell commits to the nearest one. Annealing β from small to large lets the constraints act first and commits late.

3 · A 4×4 Sudoku solved by descending one energy

The same energy at puzzle scale, with weights set by hand rather than trained. Each of the 16 cells is a unit vector in four dimensions, with digit prototypes e₁…e₄. The evidence term pins the clues. One repulsive attention head over each cell's row, column and box pushes peers apart. The memory pulls every cell toward a digit, with β annealed. The update is the plain gradient step z ← Expz(−η grad E). The bars in each cell show its soft assignment at the current β, and a digit turns solid once the cell commits.

4 · Restarts, and why the energy can choose between them

Restarting from new random states finds the solution more often. The hard part is choosing which halted state to trust. In SFPRM every halted state has a residual near zero, so the residual cannot rank them, and the anchor angle turned out to rank them badly. With an energy the choice is natural: a wrong board violates a constraint or sits off the prototypes, so it ends higher. Picking the lowest-energy restart then works whenever any restart found the answer.

5 · What the trained models show

The same picture appears in the trained models: SFPRM against the energy model with the codebook readout on Sudoku, 500 out-of-distribution test puzzles with fewer clues than in training. First, the spectrum of the Jacobian at halted states, damped by a step η of your choice. Every eigenvalue of the energy model is real, as the theory requires, while 96% of SFPRM's form complex pairs. The energy model's eigenvalues spread far along the real axis, so it needs a small step, which is the stiffness from figure 1.

Second, accuracy against the number of restarts. For SFPRM only rules without an energy are available. For the energy model with the codebook readout, the lowest-energy restart reaches the oracle: 75% to 99% out of distribution on 4×4 and 10% to 88% on 6×6, with 64 restarts. The first energy model, without the codebook readout, shows what happens when the energy does not rank answers: selection makes things worse.
